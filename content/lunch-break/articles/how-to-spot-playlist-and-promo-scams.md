The uncomfortable part of this subject is that being careful is not sufficient.
You can pay nobody, pitch nothing, and still wake up to a stream count that does
not belong to you, because somebody else's playlist put your song somewhere you
never agreed to be.

So this is two things. It is how to read an offer before you accept it, and it
is what to do when something has already happened to you.

## The free thing people get sold

Start here, because it is the single most common sale.

**Pitching your unreleased music to Spotify's editorial playlists is free.** It
is a feature of Spotify for Artists, which is free, and you do it yourself.
Spotify asks that the music is delivered at least seven days before release so
the editors have time to listen, and you pitch one song at a time. Pitching does
not guarantee anything — but submitting seven or more days ahead does guarantee
the release lands in your followers' Release Radar, which is not nothing.

A service charging you to do that is charging you for a form you can fill in.
Some will also submit on your behalf and describe it as access. There is no
access. There is one queue.

## How bot playlists work, and why it lands on you

A bot playlist is a container that exists to be streamed by something that is
not a person. It accumulates followers that were bought, sits on a network of
accounts that play it around the clock, and sells slots to artists — or, often
enough, adds tracks nobody paid for, to look like a real playlist with real
taste.

That last behaviour is the reason an artist who did everything right can end up
in the middle of it. Your song gets added. The network streams it along with
everything else. Your numbers move.

Spotify describes artificial streaming as a stream that does not reflect genuine
listening intent, and says that when it is detected the streams do not generate
royalties, do not count toward public numbers or charts, and do not feed
recommendations — and that depending on severity, tracks can be removed from
playlists or from Spotify altogether. Since April 2024 it has also charged
labels and distributors per track where flagrant artificial streaming is found
on their content, which is why your distributor takes this seriously and why
they hear about it before you do.

The enforcement is aimed at the activity, not at proving who arranged it. That
asymmetry is the whole problem, and it is why documentation matters more than
innocence.

## Red flags in an offer

These are patterns, not accusations. No company is named here, deliberately —
names change and patterns don't.

- **It guarantees a number.** Streams, followers, placements. Nobody can
  guarantee a listener, and Spotify's guidance says services advertising streams
  in return for payment violate its terms.
- **It charges to pitch to Spotify editorial.** See above. You already have this.
- **It wants your account login.** Spotify for Artists, your distributor
  dashboard, anything. A promotion service has no reason to hold credentials,
  and account access is how a catalogue gets moved or redirected.
- **You cannot identify the curators.** Real names, real profiles, real history.
- **It will not name the playlists in advance.** If you cannot see where the
  song is going, you cannot check it.
- **The price is tied to stream counts.** Pricing by outcome only makes sense
  when the outcome can be manufactured.
- **The deliverable is a result, not work.** "Placement" is a result. "A curator
  listens and writes back" is work.
- **There is urgency.** Countdowns and last-slots messaging exist to stop you
  spending ten minutes checking.
- **There is no trace of them outside their own marketing.** Testimonials are
  free to write.

The [scam checker](/resources/tools/promo-checker) walks through these as
questions and tells you which ones you triggered and why.

## Legitimate, risky, and the line between them

It is worth being precise here, because "never pay for promotion" is both common
advice and not quite right.

**Paying for attention is normal.** Submission platforms that charge a fee to
put your song in front of a curator, a blogger or a radio programmer, where what
you are buying is a listen and a written response, are a straightforward
transaction. You paid for someone's time. Sometimes the answer is no. A publicist
you pay by the month to pitch press is the same shape: you are buying labour and
relationships, and no honest one promises coverage.

**Paying for placement is the line.** The moment the thing being sold is a
position on a playlist — a guaranteed slot, a number of adds, a stream target —
you have moved from buying work to buying a result that can only be delivered by
manufacturing it.

The test that holds up: *if this works exactly as promised, what actually
happened?* Someone listened and liked it, or something ran a script. If the
promise can only be kept by the second, the promise is the problem.

Advertising is its own category and is fine. Buying ads on Meta, TikTok or
Spotify's own ad products is buying impressions from a platform, transparently,
at a rate anyone can see.

## Is this playlist fake?

Signals, in rough order of how much weight to give them:

1. **Huge follower count, almost no engagement.** Followers are cheap to
   fabricate; saves, likes and comments are not. The gap is the clearest tell a
   playlist has on its face.
2. **The curator contacted you afterwards offering paid promotion.** The free
   placement was the demonstration.
3. **A spike concentrated in one country or city** where you have no audience
   and no reason to. Spotify specifically tells artists to report exactly this.
4. **No identifiable curator.** You also need someone to ask for a removal.
5. **Tracks with nothing in common,** or mostly by artists with no presence
   anywhere else.
6. **Listeners wildly out of proportion to saves and follows.** Real listening
   leaves a trail.

Any one of these has an innocent explanation. Three of them together is a
pattern.

## If your streams spike, or you get a warning

In order, and the order matters.

**1. Document before anything disappears.** Screenshot the playlist, its follower
count, its track list and the curator profile. Screenshot your daily stats and
your listener geography. Save the full message thread with whoever approached
you, and any receipts. Playlists get deleted and accounts vanish; your
screenshots don't.

**2. Tell your distributor, in writing, before they tell you.** Say plainly what
you did and did not pay for, and attach what you gathered. Your distributor is
the one who hears from the DSPs, and arriving with a documented account turns a
takedown into a conversation. Spotify also notes that the royalty report from
your label or distributor is the most accurate source of truth here, since
withheld streams can still show up in your own analytics.

**3. Report the playlist to Spotify**, and ask the curator to remove the track
if you can find one. Keep the request and the reply.

**4. Do not panic-buy a fix.** There is no "stream cleanup" product, no
"protection" service, and no way to pay someone to make this go away. The people
selling that found you the same way the first lot did.

**5. Then wait, and keep releasing.** This is survivable. Artists get through it
regularly, and a documented, promptly-reported incident looks completely
different from a silent one.

## The part that actually protects you

Nothing above is a defence in depth. The only real one is being an artist whose
numbers make sense: a mailing list, a modest and real audience, and growth you
can account for. When your streams are explicable, an anomaly stands out — to
you first, and to everyone else after.

That is a slower answer than any of these offers, which is precisely why the
offers exist.
