I put a record out, watched the streaming numbers do something modestly
encouraging, and assumed the money side was handled. I had a distributor. The
distributor paid me. That was the system, as far as I could tell.

It wasn't. A song is not one asset earning one royalty. It is two copyrights
earning several royalties, held by different organisations, most of which will
never contact you. My distributor was collecting one of them correctly and had
no obligation — or ability — to collect the rest.

None of this is a scandal, and nobody is hiding it. It is just that the system
was built around labels and publishers who have staff for this, and if you are
doing it yourself between a day job and dinner, nobody hands you the list.

So here is the list.

## What your distributor actually collects

Your distributor collects the master-side income from streaming and downloads,
and pays it to you minus their cut. That is the job. It is a real job and they
generally do it well.

What it is not is everything. That payment covers the *recording* on the
*interactive* services — Spotify on demand, Apple Music, the ones where a
listener picks the song. It does not cover the composition at all, and it does
not cover the recording on the services where a listener does not pick the song.
Those are separate pools of money, sitting with separate organisations, waiting
for someone to claim them.

## The two copyrights, in one paragraph

Every song you write and record is two pieces of property. There is the
**composition** — the melody, the lyrics, the thing that would still exist if
someone else recorded it. And there is the **sound recording**, also called the
master — the specific take you made on a specific day. A cover version is a new
recording of the same composition. A sync in a film licenses both, separately,
from whoever owns each.

If you wrote it and recorded it yourself, you own both, which feels like it
should make things simpler. It doesn't. It means you have to claim both, from
different places.

## Who collects what

**Your PRO — performance royalties on the composition.** Whenever the
composition is performed publicly — radio, television, a bar, a venue, and the
performance component of a stream — a performing rights organisation collects
for it. In the US that is ASCAP or BMI, and you affiliate with one of them, not
both. Joining as a writer is free at either.

**The MLC — US digital mechanicals on the composition.** The Mechanical
Licensing Collective administers the blanket mechanical licence for eligible
streaming and download services in the US. Its own FAQ is blunt about who has
to act: if your shares are not covered by a publisher or a publishing
administration service, they are *self-administered*, and you have to join The
MLC and register your works to collect. Membership is free. It is US-only.

**SoundExchange — digital performance royalties on the recording.**
SoundExchange is the organisation designated to administer the US statutory
licence for non-interactive digital services: SiriusXM, Pandora, internet radio
and the rest. The law splits that money three ways — 45% to the featured
artists, 5% to a fund for non-featured performers, and 50% to the owner of the
sound recording. If you recorded and released it yourself, you are two of those
three parties, and you register as both.

**YouTube Content ID — optionally, and carefully.** Content ID is YouTube's
matching system, and it lets you collect ad revenue when other people's videos
use your recording. You almost certainly cannot get it directly: YouTube grants
access to rights holders with a substantial body of frequently-uploaded original
material, not to individual artists who apply. The normal route is opting in
through your distributor. See the caveat below before you do.

## How to register songs with BMI or ASCAP — and the half nobody claims

Affiliating is the easy part. The part that quietly costs people money is the
publisher share.

Performance royalties are split fifty-fifty between the writer side and the
publisher side. If you are self-released and have never signed a publishing
deal, there is no publisher — and what happens to that other half depends
entirely on which PRO you joined.

**ASCAP** is explicit: if you have not signed a publishing deal and you want to
collect the publisher's share, you need to set up a publishing company with
ASCAP. It is not as heavy as it sounds — their own guidance says the
Individual/Sole Proprietor option needs a mailing address, an email address, a
US tax ID and about ten minutes, with no corporation or business name required.
At the time of writing they waive the publisher application fee if you join as
writer and publisher together, and they note that the waiver is temporary.

**BMI** works differently. Its Royalty Policy Manual states that where no
performing rights have been assigned to a publisher, the songwriters receive the
entire 200% — BMI's way of saying the writers collect both halves. A BMI writer
with no publisher is, on that rule, already whole.

Two organisations, two opposite answers to the same question. This is exactly
the kind of thing that does not survive being remembered from a forum post, so
check your own PRO's current rule rather than mine.

## How to register with The MLC

Create a member account as a self-administered songwriter, then register each
work: title, writers, and the split between them. The splits are the part that
goes wrong. They have to total 100% across all writers, and they have to agree
with what your co-writers filed. When two registrations disagree, the money does
not get split down the middle — it sits unmatched until somebody sorts it out.

This is the argument for a signed split sheet on the day you write the song,
while everyone is still cheerful about it.

## SoundExchange for independent artists

Register, then claim your recordings on both sides — the featured artist side
and the rights owner side. People register as the artist, see money arrive, and
never realise the larger half was sitting in the other account the whole time.

Worth knowing what it does not cover: this is non-interactive services only. It
is not your Spotify on-demand money, which your distributor already handles.

## Metadata is what makes money findable

Every registration above is a matching problem. The codes and names are what the
matching runs on.

- **ISRC** identifies the recording. Your distributor assigns it. Reuse it
  everywhere and never mint a second one for the same master.
- **ISWC** identifies the composition. It comes through your PRO once a work is
  registered — you do not apply for it separately.
- **Writer names** have to be spelled the same way every single time. Not your
  artist name on one and your legal name on another. Pick the form that is on
  your PRO registration and use that one, forever.
- **IPI numbers** are the identifiers your PRO issues for you as a writer and
  for your publishing entity. Put them on every registration.
- **Splits** get documented and signed at the time, not reconstructed later.

None of this is interesting. All of it is the difference between a payment and
an unmatched line in a database.

## The caveat on YouTube Content ID

Content ID claims every match automatically, and "every match" includes your
own uploads and live videos of you playing your own song. If you opt in through
a distributor, ask them to whitelist your channel first, and ask what share they
take of Content ID revenue. YouTube's own documentation notes that rights
holders who repeatedly make erroneous claims can lose access altogether, which
tells you something about how blunt the instrument is.

## What I set up, and in what order

<!-- STEFAN: this is the section readers will trust most, and it needs your actual sequence and your actual mistakes. What follows is the order that makes sense on paper — replace it with what you really did, including anything you did late or in the wrong order. -->

1. Affiliated with a PRO as a writer, before the first release rather than after.
2. Sorted out the publisher side, once I understood it was half the money.
3. Joined The MLC as a self-administered writer and registered the catalogue in
   one sitting.
4. Registered with SoundExchange, as both the artist and the rights owner.
5. Went back through every song and made the writer names and splits agree
   across all three.

Step five took longer than the first four together, which is the real lesson:
doing it at registration time costs minutes, and doing it afterwards costs an
evening per record.

## Outside the US

All of the above is the US system. Elsewhere the shape is similar and the
institutions are different — one society often handles what The MLC and a PRO
split between them here, and many countries pay **neighbouring rights**:
performance royalties on the *recording* from broadcast and public performance,
which the US does not pay at all outside the digital statutory licence.

If a meaningful share of your listening is outside the US, that is worth a
proper look, and it is beyond what I can tell you from experience. Start with
your PRO — they have reciprocal agreements with the foreign societies and can
tell you what is already being collected on your behalf.

## The short version

You are not behind because you missed something clever. You are behind because
four organisations each hold one piece and none of them will call you. The
checklist exists so you can see which pieces are yours and tick them off a song
at a time, in whatever twenty minutes you have.
