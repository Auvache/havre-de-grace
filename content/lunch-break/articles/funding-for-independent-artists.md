Funding conversations tend to collapse into one question — *where do I get money
to make the record* — when the useful question is which of four quite different
things you are asking for. They have different timelines, different costs, and
different failure modes, and mixing them up is how people end up recouping an
advance for the next four years to pay for a session they could have
crowdfunded.

This is the map. The [Funding Directory](/resources/tools/funding) has the
actual entries, with a deadline and an honest catch on each one; there is no
point repeating them here.

## Grants

**A grant is money you do not pay back, awarded on a written application.** That
is the good news and the entire pitch. The costs are somewhere else.

They are slow. An application window, a review period and a decision can span
four to six months before any money moves, which makes a grant useless for
anything you needed in March.

They are competitive in a way the award size does not always suggest. Artist
Trust's Grants for Artist Projects awards $2,500, sixty-five times, across every
discipline in Washington State. Their Arts Innovator Award is $25,000 and there
are two of them a year.

They come with obligations. Most are tied to a specific project, on a stated
timeline, with a report at the end and rules about what the money may be spent
on. MusicOregon's Echo Fund funds creative, non-performance projects — tours,
concerts and living expenses are explicitly out.

And they are gated on residency far more often than on merit. The Echo Fund
requires you to live inside Portland city limits. Artist Trust is Washington
State. The Oregon Arts Commission is Oregon. Being twenty minutes over a river
can move you out of one pool and into another.

That last point is the useful one, and it cuts both ways: **local money is the
most winnable money you will ever apply for**, because the eligible population
is tiny. National awards are judged against thousands of applications. A county
arts commission might see forty.

### Where to find the local ones

Search these, by name, in this order:

1. Your city's arts office or arts commission.
2. Your county's arts or cultural commission.
3. Your state arts agency.
4. Regional music non-profits and music-specific funds.
5. Your state arts agency's own list of funders — several maintain one, and it
   is better than anything you will assemble from search results.

In the Pacific Northwest, that shakes out as MusicOregon and the Regional Arts &
Culture Council for Portland, the Oregon Arts Commission statewide, and Artist
Trust for Washington. Note that Washington's state arts agency, ArtsWA, mostly
funds organisations rather than individuals — Artist Trust is the one that
funds individual artists directly, which is worth knowing before you spend an
afternoon on the wrong site.

## Royalty advances

**An advance is cash now against royalties you have not earned yet.** You keep
your masters, you keep your distribution relationship, and nobody takes
ownership of anything. That is what makes it genuinely different from an old
label deal, and the pitch is accurate as far as it goes.

What it is, underneath, is borrowing from yourself. The funder pays you a lump
sum and then takes your royalty income — all of it, or a defined share — until
the advance plus their fee is recouped. Nothing is forgiven if your streams
fall; the recoupment simply takes longer.

The terms vary enormously, which is why the paperwork matters more than the
headline number. beatBread, for instance, lets you set the recoupment rate, the
estimated deal length, how much income flows through to you meanwhile, and
whether new music is included — four levers that between them decide whether
this is a sensible bridge or four lean years. Amuse's Fast Forward is smaller
and more automatic, calculated from your streaming data, and its own
documentation notes that your distribution rights are locked until the advance
and fee are recovered.

There is a separate thing that looks similar and is not: a **catalogue sale**.
Duetti buys music rights outright — as its site puts it, no debt, no interest
and no recoupment, because the money is yours and the songs are theirs. That can
be the right trade. It is just not an advance, and the difference is permanent.

### Questions to ask before you sign anything

- What is the recoupment rate — what share of my income goes to you until this
  is paid off?
- How long is the term, and what happens at the end of it if the advance has
  not recouped?
- Which catalogue is included? Only what exists today, or future releases too?
- Is my distribution locked during the deal? Can I move distributors?
- What happens if my income drops by half?
- What is the total cost if this recoups exactly on schedule — the fee, as a
  number?
- Who owns the masters at every point in this? Get the answer in the contract,
  not the sales call.
- What are the reporting and audit rights on both sides?

If a question in that list gets a vague answer, that is the answer.

## Loans and emergency funds

**These are for a crisis, and they are not a recording budget.** Keeping that
line clear is most of the point of this section.

Emergency funds for musicians are real and they are worth knowing about before
you need them. MusiCares provides direct assistance for basic living costs, paid
straight to the landlord or the utility rather than to you, and asks for five
years of music industry employment or credits on at least six commercially
released recordings. Sweet Relief assists career musicians facing a serious
medical or mental health condition, or age-related hardship, with documentation
required and a threshold of half your income coming from music. The Jazz
Foundation of America's Musicians' Emergency Fund covers housing, utilities and
pro bono medical and legal help for musicians who have made a living playing
blues, jazz and roots music.

Look these up now and put them somewhere you will find them. The worst time to
learn how an application works is the week you need it to have already worked.

Taking out an ordinary consumer loan to fund a record is a different
conversation, and it is one for a financial adviser rather than a songwriter
with a website.

## Fan funding

**Your own audience, paying up front.** You keep everything — ownership,
control, the mailing list — and the only cost is a platform fee and the work.

The platform fees are knowable, so know them. Kickstarter takes 5% of funds
raised plus payment processing of 3% + $0.30 per pledge, and funding is
all-or-nothing: miss the goal and you receive nothing at all. Patreon takes 10%
of what you earn, plus payment processing, currency conversion and payout fees.
Bandcamp takes 15% on digital, dropping to 10% once you have passed $5,000 in
sales in the past twelve months, and 10% on physical goods, with payment
processing on top.

The real costs are not the fees. They are that this only works with an audience
you already have — a campaign does not create one — and that every reward you
promise is a debt. Vinyl is the classic version of this: you raise the money,
the plant quotes six months, the shipping estimate was wrong, and eighteen
months later you are still packing boxes for a record that is already old to
you. Promise less than you think you can deliver.

## Which one, then

Roughly:

- **Making a record, no deadline:** grants and fan funding. Start applying six
  months before you need it.
- **Making a record, needs to happen now, you have real streaming income:** an
  advance, with the term sheet read twice.
- **Making a record, needs to happen now, no streaming income:** neither. Make
  a cheaper record.
- **Life has gone wrong:** emergency funds, today.

The [directory](/resources/tools/funding) filters by type and region and tells
you which deadlines have passed. Everything in it was checked against the
organisation's own site, and every entry says what it costs you.
