Every platform you build an audience on is rented. That is not a complaint about
any of them in particular — it is just the arrangement. You are allowed to
gather people there, and the platform decides how many of them see you, and it
can change that decision at any time for reasons that have nothing to do with
you.

A mailing list is the one exception. It is a list of people who said yes, that
you hold, that goes where you go.

## Reach you rent versus reach you own

The difference is easiest to see in what happens when something goes wrong.

A platform changes what it shows. Your posts reach a fraction of the people who
followed you, and there is no appeal, no explanation and nobody to ask. A
platform shuts down or becomes somewhere you no longer want to be, and the
audience does not come with you — you start again, and you ask the people who
already follow you to follow you somewhere else, using the reach that has just
been throttled.

None of that touches a list of email addresses. It sits in an export you can
download. If your email provider raises its prices or goes under, you move the
file. The relationship survives the infrastructure.

## It is smaller than you expect, and it matters more

The number will be humbling at first, and it is worth resetting the comparison
before you look at it. A few hundred people who chose to hear from you directly
is not the small version of a few thousand followers. It is a different thing.
Those are people who typed an address and confirmed it, which is a far higher
bar than a tap, and they hear from you whether or not any algorithm agrees.

This is also the audience that funds things. Pre-orders, crowdfunding, a
show in a city you were not sure about — those all run on people who already
said yes, and the list is the only place they are gathered somewhere you
control. If you ever read the [funding article](/resources/funding-for-independent-artists),
this is the quiet dependency under the fan-funding section: a campaign does not
create an audience, it converts one.

## The low-effort version

There is a version of this that costs hours a month and generates guilt. Skip
it. The version that works when your evenings are already spoken for:

**One form, somewhere people land.** Your own site, on the page people actually
arrive at. Say what they will get and roughly how often, because the honest
answer — "a few times a year, when there's a song" — is more appealing than the
implied alternative.

**Mention it where you already are.** The link in your profiles, a line at the
end of a post, once from a stage. Not a campaign; a habit.

**Write one thing per email.** The failure mode is a roundup: five updates,
assembled over an hour, asking the reader to work out which part is for them.
One song, one date, one story about how something got written. Then stop.

**Send before you need something.** A list that only hears from you when there
is a thing to buy learns exactly what it is for. A list that heard from you in
February about a song you were stuck on will read the one in June about a
record.

**Decide the frequency once.** Four times a year, put in the calendar, is a
decision you make once instead of a guilt you carry continuously. It is also
plenty.

## What to send when nothing is happening

Most of the year, nothing is happening, which is where most artist mailing lists
quietly die.

The things that are actually interesting when there is no release: what a song
was originally about before it changed, a rough take of something that will
never come out, a record you have not stopped playing, why you cut a verse, a
photograph from the room where you tracked something. None of that requires news.
All of it is the reason someone gave you their address rather than a follow.

The one rule is that it has to sound like a person. An email that reads like a
press release gets the response a press release gets.

## The uncomfortable bit

You have to ask. Repeatedly, in public, in a way that feels like promoting
yourself, which is the part most songwriters would rather skip — and skipping it
is why most lists stay at forty people for three years.

It helps to remember what is actually being offered. You are not extracting
something. You are offering the people who already like the songs a way to hear
about the next one without depending on a feed to tell them. Put like that, it is
a service, and it is fine to mention it more than once.
